## 4. Modus operandi (what the captures prove)

- **Reactive poisoner, not a flooder.** All 20 captured fake "`10.255.31.254 is-at xx-xx-xx-xx-xx-xx`" replies landed **0–1 ms after a victim asked** "who has the gateway?". Never unsolicited. It races the real router on every ARP request; whoever answers last wins the victim's ARP cache.
- **Blackholes.** Victim traffic sent to it gets **nothing back** (71 packets at 05:34 on 10-28, zero returns). It answers ARP for the gateway IP but does **not** answer pings to it → it doesn't really hold the IP, it only lies about owning it. Pure cut-off.
- **Join fingerprint.** On (re)connect it emits **8× `who-has 10.255.31.254 tell 0.0.0.0`** in ~1.5 s, then one `tell 10.255.18.145` (gratuitous ARP announce). 16 such bursts recorded. This is standard Android Wi-Fi DHCP reconnect behavior — i.e. it marks when the phone rejoins the AP. No other device on the LAN shows this exact pattern, so it's a reliable "attacker is online" trigger.
- **Victim look-ups.** Seen `who-has <victim> tell 10.255.18.145` for 27 distinct resident IPs, usually 0.1–7 s after that victim first appeared on the LAN. Retries ~0.77 s when unanswered. (Caveat: a normal Android also resolves newcomers for mDNS; the *solid* proof is the fake gateway replies + blackhole, not these look-ups.)
